\begin{center}
  {\fontsize{20}{26}\selectfont\bfseries ABSTRACT}
\end{center}

\vspace{2\baselineskip}

The proliferation of software-defined vehicle (SDV) platforms has opened an
opportunity to combine modern deep reinforcement learning (RL) with real
automotive runtime stacks. This thesis presents \textbf{SHILATE} --- a
complete, open-source pipeline that trains a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
agent inside a Unity physics simulation and deploys the resulting policy onto an
Eclipse Leda SDV stack, bridging the gap between laboratory RL research and
realistic vehicle software deployment.

The simulation component is built in Unity 2022 LTS. A single rear-wheel-drive
vehicle navigates a circular obstacle course instrumented with a 21-ray
LiDAR-style sensor. The training bridge computes a shaped reward signal from
progress, collision events, and episode milestones, and communicates with the
Python training process exclusively over MQTT, keeping the two subsystems fully
decoupled. A dedicated Editor window (\emph{SHILATE Training Controller})
provides real-time health monitoring, metric graphs, and one-click training
control without leaving the Unity Editor.

The Python training stack wraps the simulation in a Gymnasium-compliant
environment, configures Stable-Baselines3 PPO with a two-layer actor-critic
network, and streams structured telemetry back to the Editor. Trained
checkpoints are subsequently deployed to the Eclipse Leda SDV image via a
containerised MQTT-to-Kuksa bridge and a Velocitas vehicle application that
enforces operational limits (overspeed, RPM redline) on the running agent.

Experimental results demonstrate that the PPO agent converges reliably on the
obstacle course within 100k interaction steps and that the end-to-end deployment
chain correctly propagates vehicle telemetry from the simulation through the
VSS data layer to the Velocitas safety monitor. The project shows that an
integrated sim-to-SDV pipeline is achievable with commodity open-source
tooling, and identifies curriculum learning and multi-environment
parallelisation as natural extensions for future work.

\textbf{Keywords:} reinforcement learning, software-defined vehicle, Unity
simulation, PPO, MQTT, Eclipse Leda, Kuksa, Stable-Baselines3, Gymnasium.

\subsection*{1.1 \quad MOTIVATION AND CONTEXT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{1.1 \quad Motivation and Context}

\indent The automotive industry is undergoing a fundamental architectural shift.
Traditional vehicles are increasingly giving way to software-defined vehicles
(SDVs) --- platforms in which electronic control units are consolidated onto
high-performance compute hardware and the vehicle's behaviour is governed by
software layers that can be updated over the air. This transformation enables
capabilities that were previously inconceivable: dynamic reconfiguration of
driving characteristics, continuous safety-critical updates without a workshop
visit, and the integration of machine-learning-based components directly into
the vehicle runtime.

At the same time, deep reinforcement learning (RL) has matured into a practical
engineering tool. Algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
\cite{Schulman2017} achieve human-competitive or superhuman performance in
continuous control tasks ranging from robotic locomotion to game playing, with
comparatively modest tuning effort. The natural question that arises is
therefore: \emph{how can an RL-trained policy be moved, end-to-end, from a
training simulation onto an SDV runtime stack in a reproducible and maintainable
way?}

This question is not merely academic. Automotive-grade deployment requires that
a trained model run inside a standardised software container, interact with
vehicle signals through a well-defined data layer, and remain subject to
functional-safety monitors that can intervene when the model operates outside
its intended envelope. Bridging this gap between laboratory RL and production
SDV middleware is the core motivation behind the work presented in this thesis.

\subsection*{1.2 \quad PROBLEM STATEMENT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{1.2 \quad Problem Statement}

\indent Existing RL research in the autonomous driving domain concentrates
almost exclusively on training performance --- reward curves, sample efficiency,
and policy robustness. The software engineering challenge of taking a trained
checkpoint and deploying it as a first-class citizen of an SDV runtime stack is
largely unexplored in the open-source community. Concretely, the following
sub-problems remain unaddressed in an integrated, open-source pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.5cm, label=\textbf{P\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{Simulation-to-middleware coupling.} A physics simulator and an
    SDV middleware stack speak fundamentally different protocols; a
    communication bridge that is both low-latency and decoupled from either
    system is needed.
  \item \textbf{Standardised vehicle-signal representation.} Raw simulator
    outputs (floating-point physics quantities) must be mapped onto
    industry-standard vehicle signal specifications (VSS) so that the SDV
    safety layer can interpret them without simulator-specific knowledge.
  \item \textbf{Operational safety monitoring.} A deployed RL policy may
    extrapolate outside its training distribution; a lightweight, declarative
    safety monitor must be able to override the policy's actions in real time.
  \item \textbf{Reproducible training workflow.} The entire pipeline --- from
    launching the simulation to saving a deployable checkpoint --- must be
    reproducible from a single control surface without manual orchestration.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{1.3 \quad OBJECTIVES}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{1.3 \quad Objectives}

\indent This thesis addresses the above problems through the design, implementation, and
evaluation of \textbf{SHILATE} (Software-defined-vehicle Hardware-In-the-Loop
Autonomous Training Environment). The specific objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.5cm, label=\textbf{O\arabic*.}]
  \item Design and implement a Unity-based physics simulation of a rear-wheel-drive
    vehicle navigating an instrumented obstacle course.
  \item Define a shaped reward function that guides a PPO agent to complete
    laps reliably and safely, and expose a Gymnasium-compliant Python interface
    over MQTT.
  \item Implement a real-time health-monitoring and training-control layer inside
    the Unity Editor so that a researcher can observe and intervene in a training
    run without leaving the development environment.
  \item Construct an SDV deployment chain using Eclipse Leda, the Kuksa vehicle
    signal databroker, and a Velocitas vehicle application that enforces
    operational limits on the running policy.
  \item Evaluate the pipeline quantitatively: training convergence, inference
    lap performance, and end-to-end signal propagation through the SDV stack.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{1.4 \quad THESIS STRUCTURE}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{1.4 \quad Thesis Structure}

\indent The remainder of this document is organised as follows. Chapter~2 reviews the
theoretical background in reinforcement learning, SDV middleware, and

\subsection*{2.1 \quad REINFORCEMENT LEARNING FUNDAMENTALS}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{2.1 \quad Reinforcement Learning Fundamentals}

\indent Reinforcement learning is a computational framework for sequential decision
making under uncertainty \cite{Sutton2018}. An agent interacts with an
environment modelled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined by the tuple
$(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$, where $\mathcal{S}$ is the state
space, $\mathcal{A}$ is the action space, $P: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A}
\times \mathcal{S} \rightarrow [0,1]$ is the transition kernel, $R:
\mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is the reward function,
and $\gamma \in [0,1)$ is the discount factor. The agent's goal is to learn a
policy $\pi: \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathcal{A}$ (or a distribution over
actions) that maximises the expected discounted return $G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\gamma^k R_{t+k+1}$.

\textbf{Policy gradient methods} optimise the policy directly by computing the
gradient of the expected return with respect to the policy parameters
$\theta$. The foundational result is the policy gradient theorem:

\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}
    \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s) \cdot Q^{\pi_\theta}(s, a) \right],
\end{equation}

\noindent where $Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a)$ is the action-value function under the
current policy. In practice, a learned value function (critic) $V_\phi(s)$
is used to compute the advantage $A(s,a) = Q(s,a) - V_\phi(s)$, which
reduces gradient variance without introducing bias.

\textbf{Actor-critic} architectures maintain both a policy network (actor) and a
value network (critic), trained simultaneously. This combination underpins
most modern continuous-control RL algorithms, including the one used in this
work.

\subsection*{2.2 \quad PROXIMAL POLICY OPTIMIZATION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{2.2 \quad Proximal Policy Optimization}

\indent Proximal Policy Optimization \cite{Schulman2017} is a policy gradient algorithm
designed to take the largest possible improvement step on a policy without
destabilising training. It addresses the instability of earlier methods such
as TRPO by replacing the constrained optimisation with a clipped surrogate
objective that is cheap to compute and easy to implement:

\begin{equation}
  L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[
    \min\!\left( r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t,\;
      \text{clip}\!\left(r_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\right)\hat{A}_t
    \right)
  \right],
\end{equation}

\noindent where $r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a_t|s_t)$
is the probability ratio between the updated and old policies, $\hat{A}_t$ is
the estimated advantage at time step $t$, and $\epsilon$ is a clipping
hyperparameter (typically $0.2$).

The full training objective combines the clipped policy loss, a value function
regression term, and an entropy bonus:
\begin{equation}
  L(\theta) = L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)
            - c_1 L^{\text{VF}}(\theta)
            + c_2 H[\pi_\theta(\cdot|s_t)],
\end{equation}

\noindent where $c_1$ and $c_2$ are scaling coefficients. PPO is well suited to
the vehicle control domain because: (i) the continuous action space
(steer, throttle, brake) maps naturally onto the Gaussian policy it employs;
(ii) it is on-policy, avoiding the distribution-shift issues that complicate
off-policy methods in safety-critical settings; and (iii) its
data-collection strategy (rollout buffers of fixed length) integrates
cleanly with a simulation that runs at real time in a separate process.

The Stable-Baselines3 library \cite{Raffin2021} provides the PPO implementation
used in this project; it has been validated against reference implementations
across a range of benchmark tasks.

\subsection*{2.3 \quad SIMULATION-TO-REAL TRANSFER IN AUTONOMOUS DRIVING}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{2.3 \quad Simulation-to-Real Transfer in Autonomous Driving}

\indent Training autonomous driving policies in simulation rather than on physical
hardware is standard practice, motivated by safety, cost, and the ability to
reset episodes deterministically \cite{Kiran2021}. The primary risk is the
\emph{sim-to-real gap}: discrepancies between simulated and real sensor readings,
physics, and environmental conditions cause policies that perform well in
simulation to degrade when deployed.

Prominent simulation platforms include CARLA \cite{Dosovitskiy2017}, an
Unreal-Engine-based open-source simulator that models urban driving at high
visual fidelity, and the Unity ML-Agents Toolkit \cite{Juliani2020}, which
provides a general-purpose RL training framework tightly integrated with the
Unity Editor. SHILATE uses raw Unity physics without ML-Agents, for two
reasons: first, it avoids an opaque intermediate API layer and gives direct
control over the observation and reward structures; second, and more
importantly, the training process lives entirely in Python and communicates
through a standard MQTT broker, which is precisely the protocol used by the
target SDV deployment stack. The simulation therefore serves simultaneously as
a training environment and as an emulation of the vehicle bus interface.

\subsection*{2.4 \quad SOFTWARE-DEFINED VEHICLES AND THE VSS DATA MODEL}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{2.4 \quad Software-Defined Vehicles and the VSS Data Model}

\indent The COVESA Vehicle Signal Specification (VSS) \cite{COVESA2023} is a
hierarchical, vendor-neutral data model for vehicle signals, adopted by the
Eclipse SDV working group as the canonical representation for in-vehicle data.
VSS paths follow a dotted-name convention, e.g., \texttt{Vehicle.Speed} (km/h),
\texttt{Vehicle.Powertrain.CombustionEngine.Speed} (RPM), and
\texttt{Vehicle.Chassis.SteeringWheel.Angle} (degrees).

The Eclipse Kuksa databroker \cite{EclipseKuksa2024} implements a VSS-aligned
gRPC/REST server that acts as the vehicle signal hub. Producers (e.g., an
MQTT bridge receiving simulator telemetry) write to VSS paths; consumers (e.g.,
a safety application) subscribe to changes or poll current values. This
decoupled producer/consumer model is a cornerstone of the SDV architecture
used in this project.

Eclipse Velocitas \cite{EclipseVelocitas2024} builds on top of Kuksa by
providing a vehicle-application SDK and a deployment scaffold. A Velocitas app
subscribes to VSS signals, implements business logic, and publishes alerts or
control signals back to the vehicle bus. In SHILATE, the Velocitas application
monitors the live RL agent for speed and RPM limit violations.

\subsection*{2.5 \quad MQTT AND PUBLISH-SUBSCRIBE MESSAGING}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{2.5 \quad MQTT and Publish-Subscribe Messaging}

\indent MQTT 3.1.1 \cite{OASIS2014} is a lightweight publish-subscribe protocol
designed for constrained IoT environments. A central broker (Mosquitto, in
this project) decouples producers and consumers: a publisher pushes a message
to a topic string, and all subscribers to that topic receive it, with no
direct network coupling between the two parties. The protocol supports three
Quality-of-Service (QoS) levels; SHILATE uses QoS 0 (at-most-once delivery)
throughout, which minimises latency at the cost of possible message loss ---
an acceptable trade-off in a training loop where the next observation replaces
the current one within tens of milliseconds.

The MQTT protocol is also natively supported by the Eclipse Leda SDV image,
which ships Mosquitto as a core service. This means the same communication
fabric used during simulation training can be reused without modification on
the deployment target, eliminating the need for a protocol-translation layer
between training and production.

\subsection*{2.6 \quad RELATED WORK}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{2.6 \quad Related Work}

\indent A substantial body of work addresses RL for autonomous driving within
simulation \cite{Kiran2021}. OpenAI's \texttt{gym-car-racing} environment
\cite{Brockman2016} provides a top-down racetrack with pixel observations; it
is widely used as a benchmark but offers no path to real vehicle deployment.
CARLA-based RL studies \cite{Dosovitskiy2017} achieve high visual realism but
operate as closed systems with no integration to SDV middleware.

The Unity ML-Agents toolkit \cite{Juliani2020} enables RL training within
Unity but couples the Python trainer tightly to Unity's \texttt{side-channel}
API, making it difficult to reuse the same Python environment against a
physical vehicle. SHILATE deliberately avoids this coupling: the Python
environment communicates with Unity only through MQTT, the same protocol the
Leda SDV image uses for vehicle bus telemetry.

From the SDV side, the Eclipse Kuksa and Velocitas communities have
demonstrated containerised vehicle applications that read VSS signals and
enforce policies, but without an RL training pipeline attached. SHILATE
contributes the missing link: a training-to-deployment chain that is agnostic
to both the simulation engine and the SDV stack as long as both speak MQTT
and VSS.

\subsection*{3.1 \quad HIGH-LEVEL OVERVIEW}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{3.1 \quad High-Level Overview}

\indent SHILATE is structured as three loosely coupled tiers, each running as an
independent process or container, and communicating exclusively through a
Mosquitto MQTT broker. This design ensures that any tier can be replaced or
upgraded without modifying the others, and that the same communication fabric
used during training is available unchanged on the deployment target.

\textbf{Tier 1 — Unity Simulation.} A Unity 2022 LTS Editor instance runs the
vehicle physics, the obstacle course, and the sensor model. It publishes
observations and reward signals to MQTT and consumes control commands from the
same broker. The Unity process also hosts the Training Controller Editor
window, which provides a developer-facing UI for health monitoring and
training control.

\textbf{Tier 2 — Python RL Trainer.} A Python process (managed by the Training
Controller) wraps the MQTT interface in a Gymnasium-compliant environment,
instantiates a Stable-Baselines3 PPO agent, and runs the training loop. It
emits structured telemetry back through the MQTT broker so the Editor window
can display live metrics. After training, the same Python process can be
re-invoked in inference mode with a saved checkpoint.

\textbf{Tier 3 — Eclipse Leda SDV Stack.} The Eclipse Leda SDV image runs on
QEMU (or physical hardware). It hosts a Mosquitto broker, a Kuksa databroker,
an \texttt{mqtt-kuksa-feeder} container that maps MQTT vehicle telemetry to VSS
paths, and a Velocitas vehicle application that monitors operational limits.
During deployment the Python inference container is also launched within Leda
via the Kanto container manager.

\subsection*{3.2 \quad COMMUNICATION BACKBONE}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{3.2 \quad Communication Backbone}

\indent All inter-tier communication passes through a single Mosquitto MQTT 3.1.1
broker. The topic namespace uses the fixed prefix \texttt{env0/}, reflecting the
single-environment design of the current system. Table~\ref{tab:mqtt-topics}
summarises the complete topic set.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{MQTT topic map for the SHILATE training loop.}

  \vspace{0.5\baselineskip}
  \begin{tabular}{>{\ttfamily}lll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Topic} & \textbf{Direction} & \textbf{Payload} \\

QoS 0 (at-most-once delivery) is used throughout. Given that observations
arrive at 10 Hz and each new observation makes the previous one irrelevant,
occasional message loss is harmless and the absence of acknowledgement
handshakes reduces loop latency significantly compared to QoS 1 or 2.

\subsection*{3.3 \quad TRAINING DATA-FLOW}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{3.3 \quad Training Data-Flow}

\indent Each training step follows a synchronised request-response pattern over MQTT.
The Python environment calls \texttt{step(action)}, which:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.5cm]
  \item Publishes \texttt{steer}, \texttt{throttle}, and \texttt{brake} to the
    three control topics.
  \item Blocks on a threading event until Unity publishes a new
    \texttt{training/obs} message (or a 5-second timeout elapses).
  \item Reads the co-published \texttt{training/reward} and
    \texttt{training/done} values.
  \item Returns \texttt{(obs, reward, terminated, truncated, info)} to
    Stable-Baselines3.
\end{enumerate}

This cycle runs at the Unity physics rate (approximately 50 Hz wall-clock),
throttled by the 10 Hz sensor publication interval. The PPO rollout buffer
accumulates 2048 such transitions before a gradient update is performed.

\subsection*{3.4 \quad CONFIGURATION MANAGEMENT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{3.4 \quad Configuration Management}

\indent Configuration is split across three locations, each serving a different
consumer:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{config.json}} (repository root): holds \texttt{model.ray\_count}
    (default 9 in the root copy; the simulation uses 21). Both the Python trainer
    and the inference script read this file through \texttt{config\_loader.py},
    which falls back to the hard-coded default if the key is absent or the file
    is malformed.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{TrainingSettings.asset}} (Unity ScriptableObject): persists
    the training knobs that a researcher configures through the Editor window ---
    MQTT host/port, Python virtual-environment path, script paths, PPO
    hyperparameters, checkpoint save location, and an optional resume-model path.
    Unity's asset serialisation keeps these settings across Editor restarts
    without requiring a configuration file on disk.
  \item \textbf{CLI flags} (\texttt{train.py}): the same hyperparameters exposed in
    the TrainingSettings UI are passed as command-line arguments when
    \texttt{PythonProcessManager} spawns the training script, so the Python
    process is entirely stateless with respect to the Unity Editor.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{3.5 \quad DEPLOYMENT ARCHITECTURE ON ECLIPSE LEDA}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{3.5 \quad Deployment Architecture on Eclipse Leda}

\indent When a trained checkpoint is to be deployed onto the Leda image, three
containers are active simultaneously:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.5cm]
  \item \textbf{\texttt{leda-controller}} (inference mode): loaded with the
    \texttt{best\_model.zip} checkpoint; subscribes to \texttt{env0/vehicle/training/obs}
    and publishes control commands.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{mqtt-kuksa-feeder}}: bridges \texttt{env0/vehicle/\#} telemetry
    topics to VSS paths in the Kuksa databroker via gRPC.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{velocitas-app}}: a Velocitas vehicle application that subscribes
    to \texttt{Vehicle.Speed} and \texttt{Vehicle.Powertrain.CombustionEngine.Speed}
    in the Kuksa databroker and publishes alerts to
    \texttt{leda/command/alert} when thresholds are exceeded.
\end{enumerate}

Each container is described by a Kanto container manifest
(\texttt{kanto-manifest.json}), which specifies the OCI image reference,
environment variables, and restart policy. The provisioning script
\texttt{leda/provision-device.sh} copies manifests to the Leda device and
triggers Kanto to pull and start the containers.

\subsection*{4.1 \quad SCENE CONSTRUCTION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{4.1 \quad Scene Construction}

\indent The simulation scene is constructed programmatically at runtime rather than
authored in the Unity Editor. \texttt{RuntimeSceneBuilder.cs} runs on scene
load and checks whether the required game objects are present; if they are
absent, it invokes \texttt{CarFactory} to spawn the full vehicle hierarchy and
\texttt{SpawnObstacleCourse} to instantiate the track. This approach guarantees
that the scene is always in a well-defined initial state regardless of how the
Editor project was last saved.

The obstacle course is a circular ring generated by \texttt{ObstacleCourse.cs}.
The ring is bounded by two concentric cylindrical walls: the inner wall has a
radius of 25 metres and the outer wall a radius of 40 metres, giving a track
width of 15 metres. Twelve obstacle objects are distributed around the ring at
equal angular intervals. Wall height is 3 metres, sufficient to be detected by
the vehicle's raycasts but low enough to avoid occluding the camera. The vehicle
is spawned at position $(32.5, 0.5, 0)$ --- on the track centreline, facing the
initial direction of travel.

\subsection*{4.2 \quad VEHICLE PHYSICS MODEL}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{4.2 \quad Vehicle Physics Model}

\indent The vehicle is a rear-wheel-drive configuration assembled by
\texttt{CarFactory.cs}. The chassis mass is 1500 kg. Four \texttt{WheelCollider}
components provide the physics contact model, using Unity's built-in
Pacejka-inspired tyre model. Key parameters are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:vehicle-params}.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Key vehicle physics parameters.}
  \label{tab:vehicle-params}
  \vspace{0.5\baselineskip}
  \begin{tabular}{lrl}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Value} & \textbf{Unit} \\
    \midrule
    Chassis mass           & 1500   & kg \\
    Maximum motor torque   & 450    & Nm \\
    Maximum steer angle    & 35     & degrees \\
    Final drive ratio      & 9.0    & -- \\
    Maximum brake torque   & 3000   & Nm \\
    Regenerative brake     & 150    & Nm \\
    Creep enabled          & true   & -- \\
    Maximum speed          & 150    & km/h \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\texttt{VehicleController.cs} translates the three-component action vector
$[a_\text{steer}, a_\text{throttle}, a_\text{brake}]$ received from
\texttt{RemoteDriveInput.cs} into individual WheelCollider motor torques and
steer angles. A creep behaviour applies a small constant torque in drive gear
when both throttle and brake inputs are near zero, mirroring the behaviour of a
real automatic-transmission vehicle. Regenerative braking applies a torque
opposing wheel rotation whenever the throttle is released, and is modelled as a
constant regen torque rather than a speed-proportional term for simplicity.

\subsection*{4.3 \quad PERCEPTION LAYER}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{4.3 \quad Perception Layer}

\indent The vehicle's sole sensor is a LiDAR-inspired raycast fan implemented in
\texttt{RaycastSensor.cs}. Twenty-one rays are cast in the vehicle's horizontal
plane, spanning a $120^{\circ}$ arc centred on the forward axis. The ray length
is 50 metres. Each ray returns a normalised distance $d_i \in [0, 1]$, where
$0$ indicates contact at the vehicle origin and $1$ indicates no contact within
50 metres (the ray does not hit anything within range). The sensor publishes the
full ray array to \texttt{env0/vehicle/training/obs} at 10 Hz.

The observation vector received by the Python environment is:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{o} = \left[ d_0,\, d_1,\, \ldots,\, d_{N-1},\,
                      \frac{v}{v_{\max}},\,
                      \frac{\delta + 1}{2} \right] \in \mathbb{R}^{N+2},
  \label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $N$ is the configured ray count (read from \texttt{config.json}),
$v$ is the current vehicle speed in km/h, $v_{\max} = 150$ km/h, and $\delta$
is the current steer angle normalised to $[-1, 1]$. The steer component is
rescaled to $[0, 1]$ for consistency with the ray distances. All elements of
$\mathbf{o}$ therefore lie in $[0, 1]$, which simplifies network initialisation.

\subsection*{4.4 \quad REWARD FUNCTION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{4.4 \quad Reward Function}

\indent The reward function is the central mechanism through which the agent learns
to navigate the course. It is computed in \texttt{TrainingBridge.cs} and must
strike a balance between encouraging forward progress, penalising unsafe
interactions with the environment, and providing sufficiently informative
milestones to overcome sparse-reward traps.

The per-step reward at time $t$ is:

\begin{equation}
  r_t = r_{\text{progress}} + r_{\text{collision}} + r_{\text{milestone}},
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}

\noindent where each term is defined as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Progress reward}: $r_{\text{progress}} = c_p \cdot \Delta p_t$,
    where $\Delta p_t$ is the signed change in track progress (checkpoints
    passed) since the last step, and $c_p = 2$. Progress is computed by
    \texttt{ObstacleCourse.cs} as the number of discrete waypoints the vehicle
    has passed in the forward direction.
  \item \textbf{Collision penalty}: $r_{\text{collision}} = c_c = -5$ on any
    time step where the vehicle's collider reports contact with a wall or
    obstacle. The penalty is applied once per contact event, not accumulated
    over the duration of contact.
  \item \textbf{Milestone bonus}: $r_{\text{milestone}} = c_f = +50$ on
    episode completion (full lap); $r_{\text{milestone}} = c_h = +25$ upon
    completing the first half-turn of the course. Both bonuses apply at most
    once per episode.
\end{itemize}

An episode terminates either when a full lap is completed, when the vehicle is
stationary for an extended period, or when the episode timeout of 60 seconds
(wall-clock, not scaled) is reached. Timeout terminations are reported to Python
as \texttt{truncated=True} so that Stable-Baselines3 bootstraps the value
estimate from the final state rather than treating the transition as a terminal
failure.

\subsection*{4.5 \quad MQTT BRIDGE IN UNITY}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{4.5 \quad MQTT Bridge in Unity}

\indent Unity does not ship a production-grade MQTT client. SHILATE includes a
minimal custom \texttt{MqttClient.cs} that implements the MQTT 3.1.1 wire
protocol \cite{OASIS2014} natively in C\# without external dependencies,
keeping the Unity project self-contained. The client handles \texttt{CONNECT},
\texttt{PUBLISH}, \texttt{SUBSCRIBE}, \texttt{PINGREQ/PINGRESP}, and
\texttt{DISCONNECT} packet types, with a configurable keep-alive of 30 seconds.

\texttt{LedaBroker.cs} sits above \texttt{MqttClient.cs} and provides the
application-facing API. It is configured via \texttt{LedaBroker.Configure(host,
port, "env0")} at startup, publishes vehicle telemetry from
\texttt{VehicleTelemetryBridge.cs} at 10 Hz, and receives control commands from
\texttt{RemoteDriveInput.cs}. A Python heartbeat timeout mechanism detects when
the Python process has stopped sending control commands (using a 5-second
threshold) and stops applying actions, leaving the vehicle stationary until the
Python process reconnects.

\subsection*{4.6 \quad TRAINING CONTROLLER EDITOR WINDOW}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{4.6 \quad Training Controller Editor Window}

\indent The SHILATE Training Controller (\texttt{SHILATE} $\to$ \texttt{Training
Controller}) is an EditorWindow that eliminates the need for manual terminal
management during a training session. Its layout from top to bottom is:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.5cm]
  \item \textbf{Toolbar}: training duration, MQTT connection indicator (colour dot).
  \item \textbf{Health banner}: a colour-coded strip showing the current health
    state emitted by \texttt{TrainingHealthEvaluator.cs}. States are
    \emph{Idle} (grey), \emph{Healthy} (green), \emph{Warning} (yellow),
    \emph{Critical} (red), and \emph{Disconnected} (grey). The banner text
    names the triggering condition explicitly (e.g., ``No observation received
    for 5 s'').
  \item \textbf{Snapshot row}: episode count, last-episode reward, 10-episode
    rolling mean reward, and heartbeat counter.
  \item \textbf{Settings panel} (collapsible): all \texttt{TrainingSettings}
    fields exposed as GUI controls.
  \item \textbf{Metric graphs}: scrolling time-series plots of
    \texttt{rollout/ep\_rew\_mean}, \texttt{train/policy\_gradient\_loss},
    \texttt{train/value\_loss}, and \texttt{train/approx\_kl}.
  \item \textbf{Issue log}: timestamped list of every health-state transition.
  \item \textbf{Raw stdout} (collapsible): unfiltered Python process output.
  \item \textbf{Controls}: \emph{Start Training} / \emph{Run Model} / \emph{Stop}.
\end{enumerate}

The \emph{Start Training} button enters Play mode programmatically and, once
the scene is loaded and the MQTT broker connection is confirmed, launches the
Python training script with the arguments from \texttt{TrainingSettings}. This
one-click workflow ensures that the simulation and the trainer always start in
the correct order.

The health evaluator monitors five independent failure modes: MQTT
disconnection, Python process crash, observation gap exceeding 5 seconds,
heartbeat/reward gap exceeding 60 seconds, and reward collapse (more than 50\%
drop over a 10-episode window). Any triggered condition transitions the banner
to \emph{Warning} or \emph{Critical} and appends an entry to the issue log.

\subsection*{5.1 \quad GYMNASIUM ENVIRONMENT WRAPPER}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{5.1 \quad Gymnasium Environment Wrapper}

\indent The Python training pipeline is built on the Gymnasium API
\cite{Towers2024}, which defines a standard interface (\texttt{reset()},
\texttt{step()}, \texttt{observation\_space}, \texttt{action\_space}) that any
Stable-Baselines3 algorithm can consume without modification. The wrapper class
\texttt{ShilateEnv(gym.Env)} in \texttt{environment.py} translates between
this interface and the MQTT broker.

\textbf{Observation space.} As derived in Equation~\ref{eq:obs}, the
observation is a $\mathbb{R}^{N+2}$ vector where every element lies in
$[0, 1]$. The space is declared as:
\begin{equation*}
  \mathcal{O} = \text{Box}(0,\; 1,\; \text{shape}=(N+2,),\; \text{dtype}=\texttt{float32}).
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Action space.} The agent outputs three continuous values:
\begin{equation*}
  \mathcal{A} = \text{Box}\!\left([-1,\,0,\,0],\; [1,\,1,\,1],\; \text{shape}=(3,),\; \text{dtype}=\texttt{float32}\right),
\end{equation*}
corresponding to steer $\in [-1, 1]$, throttle $\in [0, 1]$, and
brake $\in [0, 1]$ respectively.

\textbf{Episode lifecycle.} \texttt{reset()} sends a \texttt{reset} command to
Unity, then blocks until either an \texttt{obs} message arrives or the 5-second
\texttt{OBS\_TIMEOUT} elapses. If the timeout fires, the episode is marked
truncated and Stable-Baselines3 bootstraps the value estimate rather than
treating the transition as terminal.

\texttt{step(action)} publishes the three action components to their respective
control topics (see Table~\ref{tab:mqtt-topics}), then blocks on a threading
event until a new \texttt{obs} arrives alongside a co-published
\texttt{reward} and \texttt{done}. The threading-event approach avoids busy
polling and allows the GIL to be released while waiting, keeping CPU usage low.

\subsection*{5.2 \quad PPO AGENT CONFIGURATION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{5.2 \quad PPO Agent Configuration}

\indent The PPO agent is instantiated from Stable-Baselines3 \cite{Raffin2021},
which in turn uses PyTorch \cite{Paszke2019} as its deep-learning backend.
The environment is wrapped in a one-slot \texttt{DummyVecEnv} as required by
the SB3 API, even though only a single environment is used.

The neural network architecture, specified via \texttt{model.py}, uses separate
actor and critic branches to allow the value function to learn a different
feature representation from the policy. Each branch is a two-layer multilayer
perceptron:
\begin{equation*}
  \pi:\; \mathbb{R}^{N+2} \xrightarrow{256} \mathbb{R}^{128} \to \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|},
  \qquad
  V:\; \mathbb{R}^{N+2} \xrightarrow{256} \mathbb{R}^{128} \to \mathbb{R}.
\end{equation*}

Default PPO hyperparameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:ppo-params}. These
values follow the recommendations of \cite{Raffin2021} for continuous-action
environments and can be overridden through the Training Controller UI or via
\texttt{train.py} CLI flags.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Default PPO hyperparameters.}
  \label{tab:ppo-params}
  \vspace{0.5\baselineskip}
  \begin{tabular}{lrl}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Default} & \textbf{CLI flag} \\
    \midrule
    Total timesteps      & 100\,000  & \texttt{--total-timesteps} \\
    Learning rate        & $3 \times 10^{-4}$ & \texttt{--learning-rate} \\
    Rollout steps ($n$)  & 2048      & \texttt{--n-steps} \\
    Batch size           & 64        & \texttt{--batch-size} \\
    Clipping ($\epsilon$)& 0.2       & (SB3 default) \\
    Discount ($\gamma$)  & 0.99      & (SB3 default) \\
    GAE $\lambda$        & 0.95      & (SB3 default) \\
    Entropy coefficient  & 0.0       & (SB3 default) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection*{5.3 \quad TRAINING LOOP AND HEALTH TELEMETRY}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{5.3 \quad Training Loop and Health Telemetry}

\indent The training loop is driven by \texttt{PPO.learn(total\_timesteps)}.
A custom \texttt{HealthCallback(BaseCallback)} is attached to the training
process and invoked at the end of each rollout. It inspects the most-recent
policy gradient loss, value loss, and KL divergence for NaN or infinite values;
if any are detected it emits a \texttt{SHILATE-HEALTH nan\_in\_losses} marker
to stdout, which \texttt{TrainingHealthEvaluator.cs} uses to transition the
Editor banner to \emph{Critical}.

All metrics are transmitted to the Unity Editor through two mechanisms:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.5cm]
  \item \textbf{SB3 tabular output.}  Stable-Baselines3 prints a formatted
    table to stdout every \texttt{n\_steps}. \texttt{TrainingMetricsParser.cs}
    parses the rows \texttt{rollout/ep\_rew\_mean},
    \texttt{train/policy\_gradient\_loss}, \texttt{train/value\_loss}, and
    \texttt{train/approx\_kl} from the raw text stream.
  \item \textbf{Structured markers.} The \texttt{emit()} helper in
    \texttt{train.py} writes lines of the form
    \texttt{SHILATE-METRIC key=value} and \texttt{SHILATE-HEALTH signal} to
    stdout, enabling the Editor parser to extract specific values without
    fragile regex matching against the SB3 table format.
\end{enumerate}

Model checkpoints are saved by a \texttt{CheckpointCallback} every
approximately 10\,000 timesteps to the configured save directory, and the
\texttt{EvalCallback} saves the best-performing checkpoint as
\texttt{best\_model.zip}.

\subsection*{5.4 \quad INFERENCE MODE}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{5.4 \quad Inference Mode}

\indent \texttt{ai\_driver.py} loads a saved \texttt{.zip} checkpoint and runs the
policy in a closed-loop inference loop. It constructs observations from the
same MQTT source as the training environment, ensuring that no changes are
required to the Unity side. The policy is evaluated with
\texttt{deterministic=True}, which disables the Gaussian exploration noise
added during training. Episode telemetry (speed, steer, throttle, brake) is
logged every 50 steps to facilitate post-run analysis. The script can run a
fixed number of episodes (\texttt{--episodes}) or loop indefinitely
(\texttt{--episodes 0}).

\subsection*{5.5 \quad CROSS-PLATFORM PROCESS MANAGEMENT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{5.5 \quad Cross-Platform Process Management}

\indent \texttt{PythonProcessManager.cs} is responsible for spawning and terminating
the Python process from within the Unity Editor. Because the Editor runs on
both Windows and Linux (WSL2 is a common development environment), the manager
must handle platform-specific virtual-environment activation. On Windows, it
invokes \texttt{cmd.exe /c} with the \texttt{Scripts\textbackslash activate}
path; on Linux/macOS, it uses \texttt{bash -c} with \texttt{bin/activate}.

Graceful shutdown is handled by sending a \texttt{SIGTERM} signal (or the
Windows equivalent Ctrl+C event) to the process group, giving the Python
process time to flush TensorBoard logs and save the final checkpoint before the
Editor exits Play mode. If the process does not terminate within a configurable
timeout, \texttt{Kill()} is called as a fallback.

\subsection*{6.1 \quad ECLIPSE LEDA OVERVIEW}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{6.1 \quad Eclipse Leda Overview}

\indent Eclipse Leda \cite{EclipseLeda2023} is an SDV distribution built on the
Yocto Project. It packages a curated set of Eclipse SDV middleware components
--- Mosquitto MQTT broker, Kuksa databroker, Kanto container manager, and
OpenTelemetry collector --- into a bootable image that can run on QEMU
(x86-64) or on supported ARM hardware such as the Raspberry Pi 4. Its primary
purpose is to serve as a reference platform for developing and validating SDV
software stacks before deploying to a real vehicle ECU.

SHILATE targets the QEMU image (\texttt{sdv-image-all-qemux86-64.wic.qcow2})
included in the repository. The \texttt{leda/run-leda.sh} script launches the
image with a bridged network interface so that the host machine and the QEMU
guest share a Mosquitto broker at \texttt{127.0.0.1:1883}.

\subsection*{6.2 \quad MQTT-TO-KUKSA BRIDGE}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{6.2 \quad MQTT-to-Kuksa Bridge}

\indent The \texttt{mqtt-kuksa-feeder} container (\texttt{leda/mqtt-kuksa-feeder/feeder.py})
subscribes to the \texttt{vehicle/\#} wildcard on the Mosquitto broker and
forwards each received message to the Kuksa databroker as a VSS path write via
gRPC. The mapping is declared in \texttt{config.json} and shown in
Table~\ref{tab:vss-map}.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{MQTT topic to VSS path mapping in the Kuksa feeder.}
  \label{tab:vss-map}
  \vspace{0.5\baselineskip}

    \toprule
    \textbf{MQTT topic} & \textbf{VSS path} \\

\subsection*{6.3 \quad VELOCITAS VEHICLE APPLICATION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{6.3 \quad Velocitas Vehicle Application}

\indent The Velocitas vehicle application (\texttt{leda/velocitas-app/app.py})
implements the operational safety monitor for the RL agent. It subscribes to
five VSS signals via the Kuksa databroker, logs a telemetry summary every
5 seconds, and enforces two hard limits:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Overspeed}: if \texttt{Vehicle.Speed} exceeds 120 km/h, the
    application publishes an alert to \texttt{leda/command/alert} with the
    payload \texttt{\{"type":"overspeed","value":<speed>\}}.
  \item \textbf{RPM redline}: if
    \texttt{Vehicle.Powertrain.CombustionEngine.Speed} exceeds 6500 RPM, an
    analogous alert is published with \texttt{\{"type":"rpm\_redline","value":<rpm>\}}.
\end{itemize}

These alerts are currently informational --- they are logged and published to
MQTT for a potential external safety controller to act upon. The architecture
is designed so that the safety monitor can be upgraded to a command-injection
path (e.g., overriding the throttle control topic) without modifying any other
component of the stack.

\subsection*{6.4 \quad CONTAINER DEPLOYMENT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{6.4 \quad Container Deployment}

\indent Both \texttt{mqtt-kuksa-feeder} and \texttt{velocitas-app} ship with
Dockerfiles that produce minimal Python 3.11 slim images. The
\texttt{leda-controller} inference container uses a Dockerfile that installs
the \texttt{requirements.txt} dependencies (PyTorch, Stable-Baselines3,
Gymnasium, Paho-MQTT) and sets the entry-point to \texttt{train.py} or
\texttt{ai\_driver.py} depending on the \texttt{MODE} environment variable.

Each container is described by a Kanto container manifest
(\texttt{kanto-manifest.json}), which specifies:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The OCI image reference (local or registry).
  \item Environment variables: \texttt{MQTT\_HOST}, \texttt{MQTT\_PORT},
    \texttt{MODE} (\texttt{train} or \texttt{infer}), \texttt{TOTAL\_TIMESTEPS},
    \texttt{MODEL\_PATH}.
  \item Restart policy: \texttt{always} for the feeder and Velocitas app;
    \texttt{no} for the inference container (which runs a fixed episode count).
\end{itemize}

Provisioning is automated by \texttt{leda/provision-device.sh}, which uses SSH
to copy manifests to the Leda device and invoke the Kanto CLI to pull and
start the containers.

\subsection*{6.5 \quad LEDA TELEMETRY BROKER}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{6.5 \quad Leda Telemetry Broker}

\indent In addition to MQTT, the Leda image runs a lightweight telemetry broker
implemented in Rust. The broker listens on TCP port 7878 and acts as a
multiplexing relay: any client that connects and sends telemetry data has that
data forwarded to all other connected clients. This enables tools such as
\texttt{netcat} or custom dashboards to observe the live telemetry stream
without subscribing to individual MQTT topics. The binary is compiled from
\texttt{telemetry\_broker.rs} using the standard Rust toolchain (\texttt{rustc
telemetry\_broker.rs}).
